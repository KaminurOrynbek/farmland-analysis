3. Analysis of Existing Systems

This diploma project aims to develop a web-based platform for the automated assessment of agricultural land conditions using satellite and aerial imagery together with deep learning and GIS technologies. To justify the need for such a platform, it is necessary to analyze existing systems operating in related areas, particularly those that use remote sensing data for agricultural land monitoring and analysis. This review makes it possible to determine what solutions are currently available, what functions they provide, and to what extent they correspond to the objectives of the present research. It also helps identify their strengths and limitations and, on this basis, define the research and practical gap that the proposed platform is intended to address.

For the purposes of this study, the reviewed systems are divided into two main groups. The first one includes commercial agromonitoring platforms used for crop monitoring and precision agriculture support. The second group includes government and land-management platforms focused on land monitoring, subsidy-related processes, and land-use control. This is a proper classification because the selected systems differ in purpose, analytical depth, and accessibility, while the proposed platform focuses on CNN-based image analysis with transfer learning and on its comparison with traditional NDVI/EVI-based approaches.

3.1 Commercial agromonitoring platforms

Commercial agromonitoring platforms are important  in the practical implementation of precision agriculture. They provide farmers, agronomists, and consultants with tools for remote crop monitoring, field observation, and operational decision-making based on satellite data and related indicators. For the purposes of this study, OneSoil and EOSDA Crop Monitoring were selected as representative examples, since both platforms demonstrate how satellite-based agricultural monitoring is already used in real practice and therefore provide an appropriate basis for comparison with the proposed web service.

3.1.1 OneSoil
OneSoil is a digital agriculture platform designed for remote crop monitoring and precision farming support. According to its official website, the platform helps users monitor plant development, track field vegetation using the NDVI index, identify problem areas, and apply precision farming technologies. OneSoil also allows users to add field boundaries detected from satellite images, analyze NDVI, growing degree-days, and accumulated precipitation charts, and use web and mobile tools for scouting and field observation. The platform is available through both web and mobile applications and is presented as an accessible digital agriculture tool for crop monitoring and field observation.
From a practical point of view, the strengths of OneSoil include its accessibility, convenient interface, and combination of several useful monitoring functions in one environment. It allows users to quickly detect potentially problematic zones, observe vegetation changes over time, and support field decisions with satellite-derived information. At the same time, from the perspective of the present research, an important limitation of OneSoil is that its public user-facing functionality is primarily centered on index-based monitoring and operational decision support. Based on its publicly described functionality, the platform emphasizes NDVI-based monitoring, weather-related indicators, and operational decision support rather than presenting direct deep-learning-based image interpretation as a core analytical function.
3.1.2 EOSDA Crop Monitoring
EOSDA Crop Monitoring is a satellite-based crop monitoring platform intended for remote farm analytics and agricultural risk management. Its official materials describe a system that enables users to monitor crop health with satellite images, detect problem zones, track vegetation changes, access weather data, receive automated risk notifications, and optimize resource use through variable-rate application (VRA) maps. The platform also provides growth-stage charts, risk maps, historical vegetation and weather analysis, and field activity tracking tools. EOSDA emphasizes the use of satellite imagery, including Sentinel-2 and other data sources, to support more informed agronomic planning and farm management. 
The strengths of EOSDA Crop Monitoring lie in its broad analytical functionality, integration of several monitoring parameters, and decision-support orientation for growers and consultants. The platform offers users a rich set of ready-to-use tools for vegetation monitoring, risk assessment, and field management. However, from the perspective of this research, its main limitation is similar to that of OneSoil: the platform is primarily built around predefined vegetation indices, growth-stage models, risk alerts, and other ready-made analytical layers. From the perspective of this research, EOSDA Crop Monitoring is primarily presented as a platform for vegetation-index monitoring, risk alerts, weather analytics, and operational farm decision support rather than as a research-oriented system for direct CNN-based interpretation of farmland imagery.
3.1.3 Interim conclusion on commercial platforms
The analysis shows that both OneSoil and EOSDA Crop Monitoring are strong examples of commercial agromonitoring platforms that successfully apply satellite data in precision agriculture. Their main advantages include remote field monitoring, visualization of crop-condition indicators, weather-related support, and tools for faster agronomic decision-making. At the same time, the two platforms have a common methodological feature: both rely mainly on vegetation indices, monitoring charts, and ready-made analytical dashboards. As a result, they do not fully solve the task addressed in the present project, namely the development of a web service for direct CNN-based analysis of farmland imagery with transfer learning and subsequent comparison with traditional NDVI/EVI-based approaches.
3.2 Government and land-management platforms
In addition to commercial agromonitoring solutions, Kazakhstan has developed government digital platforms related to agricultural land management. These systems are important for the present study because they demonstrate another direction of applying geospatial and remote sensing technologies in agriculture. Unlike commercial crop-monitoring platforms, such systems are mainly intended to support administrative processes, subsidy-related procedures, and land-use control at the state level. For this reason, AgroSpace and JerInSpectr were selected for analysis as representative examples of government and land-management platforms in Kazakhstan.
3.2.1 AgroSpace
AgroSpace is a government-oriented geospatial platform developed by JSC “National Company Kazakhstan Gharysh Sapary” in cooperation with the Ministry of Agriculture of the Republic of Kazakhstan. According to the official portal, the platform supports the Unified State Information System of Subsidizing and is intended for the transfer of geospatial data on agricultural land in order to justify subsidy applications. AgroSpace provides personalized access for agricultural producers through an electronic digital signature, creates a personal account for each user, and allows them to store personal data, view and edit cartographic information, display historical and current crop rotations, and assign an individual field number. After confirmation, the data are integrated into the electronic field map used in the subsidy system. 
From the perspective of practical value, the strengths of AgroSpace include its integration with state agricultural information systems, structured handling of field-related geospatial data, and support for formal agricultural administration processes. The platform is useful for field registration, digital record keeping, and subsidy-related workflows. However, for the purposes of the present research, its main limitation is that it is focused primarily on administrative and subsidy-support tasks rather than on intelligent image analysis. In other words, AgroSpace is not designed as a system for automated crop-condition diagnosis or for direct CNN-based interpretation of satellite or aerial imagery. 
3.2.2 JerInSpectr
JerInSpectr is another state-oriented platform related to agricultural land monitoring in Kazakhstan. Publicly available official materials describe it as a platform for the control and monitoring of the rational use of agricultural land, aimed at the automation and digitalization of business processes used to respond to cases of land-use violations. In addition, the publicly accessible login interface indicates that access is linked to an organization or farm and is performed through an electronic digital signature, which confirms its institutional and regulated mode of use. 
The main strength of JerInSpectr lies in its role in land supervision and state control. It reflects the practical use of remote sensing data and geospatial technologies for monitoring land resources and supporting regulatory decision-making. At the same time, from the perspective of this diploma project, its limitation is evident: JerInSpectr is oriented toward land-use control and administrative response, not toward crop-condition assessment, deep-learning-based image interpretation, or comparative evaluation of CNN models against vegetation-index methods. Therefore, although it is highly relevant for digital land governance, it cannot be regarded as a direct analogue of the proposed web service. 
3.2.3 Interim conclusion on government platforms
The analysis of AgroSpace and JerInSpectr shows that both platforms are important examples of the digital transformation of agricultural land management in Kazakhstan. Their strengths are connected with state-level geospatial integration, land registration, subsidy support, and control of land use. However, both systems operate mainly within an administrative and regulatory context. As a result, they do not address the task that is central to the present research, namely the development of a web service for direct CNN-based analysis of farmland imagery with transfer learning and its comparison with traditional NDVI/EVI-based approaches. 
3.3 Comparative summary and identified gap
The reviewed systems demonstrate that digital technologies are already actively used in agriculture, but they serve different groups of tasks. OneSoil and EOSDA Crop Monitoring represent commercial agromonitoring platforms that support farmers and agronomists through satellite-based field observation, vegetation monitoring, weather analytics, and operational decision-making. AgroSpace and JerInSpectr, in turn, represent government and land-management platforms that support subsidy procedures, land registration, and control of land use at the institutional level. Together, these systems show the practical relevance of remote sensing, GIS technologies, and digital tools in the agricultural sector. 
At the same time, the analysis makes it possible to identify a clear research and practical gap. The commercial platforms are mainly oriented toward vegetation-index monitoring, weather-related indicators, and ready-made analytical dashboards, while the government platforms are focused on administrative and regulatory workflows. Based on the publicly described core functionality of the reviewed systems, none of them combines in one accessible web service the following elements: direct deep-learning-based analysis of farmland imagery, adaptation of the model through transfer learning, GIS-based visualization of results, and research-oriented comparison with NDVI/EVI-based approaches.
This identified gap determines the relevance of the proposed diploma project. The developed platform is intended not only to visualize field data or support administrative procedures, but to provide a prototype intelligent web service capable of analyzing agricultural images more deeply by taking into account the spatial and textural characteristics of farmland scenes. In this way, the proposed system extends existing approaches to precision agriculture and contributes to the development of more adaptive and analytically advanced agricultural monitoring tools.

2.1 Data Collection
2.1.1. Overview of the Data Collection Strategy
In this study, the data collection strategy is designed to support two interrelated objectives: (1) providing enough labeled data to build and/or improve a machine learning model through training and/or adjusting, and (2) providing operational geospatial datasets to be used by the web service's analytical and visualization service functions. Several types of data sources are being collected and integrated into a single data collection strategy, including public benchmark datasets, multispectral satellite imagery, and vector field boundaries in GeoJSON format.
The primary methodological decision of this research is to use a transfer-learning paradigm, rather than creating an entirely new, large-scale, annotated dataset from scratch. The effort required to create a high-quality labeled dataset for remote sensing (high cost, time, etc.) is significant because the dataset must involve an expert in annotating each image/composite, and the images must be of consistent quality and cover a broad spatial extent. When it comes to the classification of agricultural land-cover using satellite imagery, this annotation effort can be extremely demanding; in order to classify an individual image/composite into the correct land-use category, the spectral characteristics of each image must be accurately assessed, and the characteristics of one image/composite cannot always be visually distinguished from another.
Transfer learning can be considered an effective approach to address the limitations associated with limited labeled data in a domain by taking advantage of representations learned from a larger-scale dataset and fine-tuning them using a smaller set of target-specific datasets (Pan & Yang, 2010). When the target dataset has been labeled and has a limited amount of labeled examples, the models built using this method have demonstrated strong performance, which is an important characteristic for agricultural remote sensing applications, where there is little to no readily available annotated data.
Furthermore, this approach is reinforced by the use of publicly available open datasets. Open benchmark datasets (e.g., EuroSAT) contain standardized, peer-reviewed, and reproducible training data that has been validated by previous studies, and can be used in place of the custom data collection process and the associated risk factors (i.e., inconsistencies in labeling, data collection sampling bias, limited reproducibility) that make it difficult to obtain consistent results or make accurate comparisons with previously published results.
From a system architecture perspective, the system operates on two primary data streams: a static dataset that contains labeled images for model training and evaluation, and a second set of dynamic images taken by remote sensing from satellite images that will be used to support the web services for operational purposes. The GeoJSON-formatted field boundary data are an additional layer of data that allows the user to perform parcel-level analyses rather than regional analyses.


2.1.2. Dataset for Training: EuroSAT
With respect to the training data component, the EuroSAT dataset serves as the primary labeled dataset for model training and fine-tuning (Helber et al., 2019). EuroSAT is a publicly available benchmark dataset created to classify land-use and land-cover using deep learning based on multispectral images of the Earth taken by the Sentinel-2 satellites (the same source as the operational part of the web service). Therefore, the training data domain matches the domain of the deployment environment, thus providing an important benefit of model generalization with regard to operational inputs, as it mitigates the risk of a domain shift occurring and thus promotes improved generalization.
Deep learning models, and in particular convolutional neural networks, require large datasets of labelled data in order to learn representations of visual features that are generalisable across multiple domains. By using a standardised benchmark dataset, such as EuroSAT, model developers can create a large enough, class-balanced collection of labelled patches of satellite images to ensure that model training will be both consistent and reproducible.
The dataset contains approximately 27,000 labeled image patches across 10 classes of land use/land cover (LULC): Annual Crop, Permanent Crop, Forest, Herbaceous Vegetation, Highway, Industrial, Pasture, Residential, River, Sea or Lake. All image patches are in the size of 64×64 pixels at a spatial resolution of 10 metres per pixel, which is the same as the actual resolution of the visible and near-infrared bands of Sentinel-2 satellites. In addition, the dataset contains a balanced number of samples per class (between 2000 and 3000).
Sentinel-2 imagery provides excellent support for classifying agricultural land use due to its ability to provide reflectance measurements across many spectral bands, including visible light as well as near infrared and shortwave infrared. These additional spectral bands provide significant information regarding vegetation density, moisture, and chlorophyll concentrations. Therefore, the EuroSAT dataset used by the pretrained model fine-tuned with the EuroSAT dataset has the capability to classify LULC classes of agricultural land use based on the spectral signatures of those lands instead of visual appearance.
The EuroSAT dataset images were reformatted to 224×224 pixel resolution before processing, so that the pixel resolutions for each image can be consistent with the input size expected by the pre-trained ResNet-50 network architecture (original image size was 64×64 pixels). The dataset is then split into training, validation, and testing subsets — using stratified sampling, to guarantee that a similar fraction of each class is represented across every split — and a training, validation, and test subset is generated with an 80:10:10 ratio. Augmentation was performed by randomly flipping and rotating the data only in the training subset to help the model generalize and prevent overfitting, and all images received normalization based on the mean and standard deviation from the ImageNet dataset, so that the input distribution to the pre-trained backbone was as expected.
However, despite these advantages, several limitations should be considered. The main issue to be solved by considering them in EuroSAT is that the original images were small (64×64 pixels), which reduces the spatial context that the model will have when training and may not be able to correctly identify field-scale patterns. Furthermore, as the EuroSAT dataset was formed by utilizing images from various parts of Europe, a geographic and climatic bias will be present in the image dataset, which leads to variance in spectral response between crops collected from the Central Asian region (e.g., Kazakhstan) and the crops collected from Europe. The proposed model is less accurate in Central Asian classification than for European classification due to differences in crop types, planting and harvest seasons, soil types and characteristics, and corresponding spectral responses of crops cultivated in Central Asia on the basis of the above features.
2.1.3. Operating Remote Sensing and Satellite Imagery Platforms
From an operational perspective, real-time satellite imagery serves as the primary data source for the system. This web-based service will rely on two types of satellites, namely: the Sentinel-2 satellite system, which will be used as the primary source of satellite imagery; and the Landsat 8/9 satellite systems, which will be used primarily for obtaining historical satellite imagery and for obtaining some conclusive imagery of current conditions.
Sentinel-2 and the Copernicus Programme
        Sentinel-2 is an ESA (European Space Agency) twin satellite that is part of the Copernicus Earth Observation Programme and provides global multispectral imagery (from 13 spectral bands) of the Earth's land surface (European Space Agency, 2021). The 13 spectral bands include (in addition to the traditional) blue light, green light, red light (VIS), and near-infrared (NIR) / shortwave infrared (SWIR). The image data from the different bands also have different spatial resolutions: 10 m for the four main VIS/NIR bands, 20 m for the six red edge/SWIR bands, and 60 m for the three bands used to correct the atmospheric effects on other visible bands at a given location in time.
As a result, the system is able to access up-to-date imagery with a revisit period of approximately 5 days, enabled by the combined coverage of the Sentinel-2A and Sentinel-2B satellites. This ensures that current imagery is available for most agricultural regions, allowing for effective monitoring of seasonal crop growth and timely detection of changes in field conditions. This temporal resolution is of paramount importance for monitoring agricultural lands as well as using timely data to inform agronomic decisions.
Google Earth Engine
In order to efficiently access and process this data, Google Earth Engine (GEE) has been chosen, as the principal platform for gaining access to satellite data within this study. Google Earth Engine is a cloud-based geospatial processing platform that will allow programmatic access to a petabyte-scale storage of historical satellite and aerial images and will include all available Sentinel-2 and Landsat imagery data in the GEE database. Use of GEE is free for both educational and research purposes; therefore, this is the most efficient option for projects within an academic setting that need access to larger amounts of geo-spatial data without the necessary institutional infrastructure for handling them.
Accessing GEE eliminates the need to keep large satellite image archives within the investigator's local computing environment and reduces or removes the need for computing power from local resources. All querying of imagery from GEE, filtering by time and location, and processing of imagery can be done entirely through use of the GEE Python API in the Cloud, with image processing results being returned as processed image tiles or can/should be exported directly to the Cloud. Within the GEE web services, Sentinel-2 imagery is queried based on the user's geographic bounds and time range of interest, and filtering (querying) is done at the time of image querying to ensure that only high-quality (generally cloud-free) images are returned for analysis.
Sentinel Hub
In addition to GEE, Sentinel Hub can be considered a complementary platform for visualization purposes. Sentinel Hub is an API-based platform that supports access to Sentinel-2 (and other multi-satellite) data products, providing a high-performance system for retrieving processed imagery tiles in common web-friendly formats. With the use of the standardized RESTful interface API, end-users can request the most current imagery, requested in real-time and immediately retrieved, and delivered to their web application (with browser-based mapping components, such as Leaflet.js). Furthermore, even though GEE is the primary backend provider of satellite imagery for this web service, additional resources can be made available through Sentinel Hub, which is especially useful for rendering satellite basemap layers on the interactive web-based frontend map.
Landsat 8 and 9
         In contrast to Sentinel-2, Landsat 8 and Landsat 9 provide imagery with lower spatial resolution (U.S. Geological Survey, 2020). Landsat 8 and Landsat 9 are not operationally integrated, but provide additional imagery to enhance the existing data sources. Landsat is designed to provide multispectral imagery at 30m spatial resolution, using a 16-day repeat cycle; this means that Landsat provides lower resolution imagery than Sentinel-2, but has a very long history (since 1972). As a primary source for multi-temporal land-use change analysis, Landsat can be used in the web application when users are trying to analyze land-use change over many years or decades.

2.1.4. Field Boundary Data
From a spatial analysis perspective, accurate field boundary data are essential. Having accurate and clearly defined spatial outlines for agricultural parcels is vital for carrying out parcel-based research and analysis, while also defining the extent to which satellite imagery will be used to assess the condition of a parcel's vegetation as per the vegetation index. For this research, the field outline is represented in GeoJSON format (the most commonly used open standard for encoding geographic features in JSON) and will be easily processed by many of the GIS tools and web-based geospatial libraries.
Users can upload GeoJSON files through the web interface. By allowing users to upload their own field boundary files, the authors of this web service have considered that the availability, format, and licensing of cadastral and parcel boundary data are very different across regions.
Uploaded geometries undergo validation to ensure topological correctness. After validation, the system calculates the area of each polygon and computes centroid coordinates for visualization and analysis.
2.1.5. Vegetation Indices: NDVI and EVI
From an analytical perspective, vegetation indices play a key role in assessing plant condition. They are quantitative spectral metrics derived from satellite imagery that characterize the state and density of vegetation cover. They exploit the differential reflectance properties of vegetation across spectral bands — particularly the strong absorption of red light and high reflectance of near-infrared radiation by healthy plant canopies — to produce normalized indicators of vegetation health that are comparable across different images, sensors, and geographic locations.
The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) is the primary vegetation indicator implemented in the current version of the web service. NDVI is calculated according to the following formula:
NDVI = (NIR − RED) / (NIR + RED)
where NIR denotes the surface reflectance value in the near-infrared spectral band, and RED denotes the reflectance in the red visible band. The normalization by the sum of both bands constrains the output to a continuous scale ranging from −1 to +1, making NDVI values directly comparable across images acquired under different illumination conditions or atmospheric states.
Values in the range of 0.6 to 0.9 correspond to dense, healthy vegetation with high photosynthetic activity, typical of mature crop canopies at peak growing season. Values between 0.2 and 0.5 indicate sparse or moderately developed vegetation, such as early-stage crops or degraded pastures. Values near zero suggest bare soil or rock surfaces, while negative values typically indicate water bodies, snow, or cloud cover. NDVI is computed within the analysis_service.py module of the backend and returned as the ndvi_value field in the API response.
EVI — Enhanced Vegetation Index
The Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) is implemented as a complementary spectral indicator to address known limitations of NDVI. EVI is calculated as:
EVI = 2.5 × (NIR − RED) / (NIR + 6×RED − 7.5×BLUE + 1)
where NIR, RED, and BLUE refer to surface reflectance values in the near-infrared, red, and blue spectral bands, respectively. The coefficients 6 and 7.5 are empirically derived correction factors that reduce the influence of atmospheric aerosols and soil background reflectance on the index value.
EVI offers two principal advantages over NDVI. First, it is less sensitive to atmospheric distortion, making it more reliable under hazy or aerosol-laden conditions. Second, EVI does not saturate as rapidly as NDVI in areas of high vegetation density, making it more discriminating in dense crop canopies where NDVI values tend to plateau. Together, NDVI and EVI provide complementary information about vegetation condition and are both included in the analytical pipeline of the web service.
2.1.6. Data Collection and Processing Pipeline
To integrate the aforementioned data sources, the workflow is structured as a multi-stage pipeline. The data collection and processing workflow is structured as a multi-stage pipeline for pulling down satellite imagery from Google Earth Engine, spatially processing the retrieved imagery, computing indices on the processed imagery, and preparing models using all the data from these images.
Stage 1 – Retrieval of satellite imagery via Google Earth Engine (GEE): Data are obtained as images using the GEE Python API. Image queries will be defined based upon the extents of the user's uploaded field boundaries (i.e., geographical boundaries), a date range specified by the user, and a maximum amount of cloud cover represented by the images retrieved for analysis. These filters yield only those images that have acceptable atmospheric quality values for use in statistical analysis.
Stage 2 – Clipping of rastered data to the user's uploaded field areas: The raster images are clipped to the boundaries of each agricultural plot according to how they were defined in the user's uploaded GeoJSON file. Clipping occurs via the Rasterio module in Python, such that each plotted area will have only the raster data contained within its boundaries (region of interest). Raster data are read by Rasterio for each raster image downloaded by GEE, and raster data pixels that fall within each agricultural parcel boundary will be retained.
In the third stage, vegetation indices — NDVI and EVI — are computed from the clipped multispectral raster data using the band arithmetic formulas described in Section 2.1.5. The computed index values are stored as additional raster layers aligned with the clipped imagery.
In the fourth stage, the imagery is preprocessed for input to the machine learning model. This includes resizing image patches to 224×224 pixels, applying normalization using ImageNet statistics, and converting the data to PyTorch tensor format. These transformations ensure compatibility with the pretrained ResNet-50 backbone used for classification.
In the fifth and final stage, the preprocessed data are passed to the machine learning model for inference. The model produces a land-cover classification for each field, which — together with the computed vegetation index values — is returned to the frontend and rendered as a map overlay with summary statistics.

2.1.7. Limitations and Challenges of Data Collection
Despite the robustness of the proposed approach, several limitations affect its reliability and scalability. Additionally, various practical challenges influence the overall performance of the system.
One of the main practical limitations associated with satellite-based operational analysis is the impact of cloud cover. Cloud cover prevents optical sensors on both Landsat and Sentinel-2 from capturing pixels; thus, there are pixels that do not exist or are contaminated due to clouds in the area over which the pixel falls. In agricultural regions such as Kazakhstan, where cloud cover is common, there may not be cloud-free images available in spring or fall; this is particularly problematic since spring and fall represent critical periods of plant growth.
Limitations of spatial resolution present another significant issue for analysing agricultural parcels that are relatively small or have irregular shapes. At a 10-meter resolution, a field of less than one hectare may be captured using somewhere between 2 and 3 pixels, therefore making the statistics from derived vegetation indices or derived classification less statistically verifiable.
Using Landsat images and Sentinel-2 images together creates difficulties because of the differences in resolution: one set has a 10m resolution, while the other has a 30m resolution. Therefore, if you wanted to compare the two datasets at a pixel level, you would first need to resample—this process introduces interpolated artifacts. To work around this challenge, data from Sentinel-2 will be used more for day-to-day operations, while data from Landsat will be solely for historical temporal comparisons.
The API rate limits and latency also create operational restrictions. There are rate limits imposed by both Google Earth Engine and Sentinel Hub, resulting in potential processing delays caused by high amounts of people utilizing the system at the same time. In order for this system to operate at a production level, there will need to be a request queue system and a cache of frequently accessed images.
Another limitation is geographic and climatic bias in EuroSAT, because the EuroSAT dataset is created based on typical growing conditions found in Europe. Since the crop types grown in Kazakhstan differ from those grown in Europe, as do soil conditions, field sizes, and the phenologies of different crops found there, this will likely result in lower accuracy for any potential fine-tuned models created to model the two regions. Therefore, it is recommended that future studies collect locally annotated training samples that represent typical growing conditions in Kazakhstan and incorporate them into existing datasets to address this limitation.

2. Literature review 
The development of digital technologies has significantly influenced modern agriculture, particularly in the monitoring and analysis of farmland. Remote sensing, geospatial data, and artificial intelligence are increasingly used to observe agricultural land and identify spatial variability. As satellite imagery and analytical platforms become more accessible, researchers have new opportunities to investigate farmland conditions across multiple scales that would not be feasible through field-based observations alone. However, existing studies often examine these areas separately, focusing on remote sensing, machine learning, or technical implementation. Therefore, a structured review of previous studies is necessary to understand the most significant developments in farmland image analysis.
This literature review examines the main directions related to farmland image analysis. It begins with the broader context of digital and precision agriculture, then discusses remote sensing in agriculture, vegetation indices and preprocessing, machine learning and deep learning for farmland image analysis, transfer learning in agricultural remote sensing, and web-based agricultural monitoring systems. Finally, it identifies the research gap that justifies the development of a web service for analyzing farmland images using transfer learning and geodata.
2.1 Digital Agriculture and Precision Agriculture
Digital agriculture provides a broad framework for improving agricultural productivity, sustainability, and decision-making through the use of data and modern technologies. Within this broader field, precision agriculture focuses on applying inputs and management practices according to the spatial and temporal variability of farmland rather than treating the entire field as uniform. This shift is important because agricultural systems are inherently heterogeneous: crop growth, soil conditions, and management needs often vary even within the same area. As a result, data-driven and site-specific approaches are increasingly viewed as more effective than traditional uniform management.
The literature identifies remote sensing, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), geospatial analysis, and intelligent computing as major components of this transition. Tsouros et al. (2019) show that UAV-based precision agriculture relies on image acquisition, vegetation analysis, photogrammetric methods, and machine learning for yield estimation, irrigation management, disease detection, and crop monitoring. From a broader perspective, Lu and Xiao (2024) argue that smart agriculture is becoming more dependent on intelligent sensing systems and advanced computational methods. Although their discussion is mainly theoretical, it highlights an important idea: modern agriculture should be understood not only as a biological and mechanical production system, but also as an information system in which data collection and interpretation support management decisions.
At the same time, the literature suggests that the transition to digital agriculture remains uneven. Moomen et al. (2024) show that although geospatial technologies have clear potential for sustainable agriculture, they remain underutilized in some agricultural regions. A broader contextual perspective is provided by He and Liu (2024), who show that agricultural land systems are influenced not only by technology, but also by economic, institutional, and geographic factors. Together, these studies indicate that digital and precision agriculture form the broad conceptual background of the present research, while also highlighting the need for practical systems that connect intelligent image analysis with geospatial information in an accessible way.
2.2 Remote Sensing in Agriculture
Remote sensing has become one of the most important tools in agricultural monitoring because it enables repeated, objective, and large-scale observation of farmland. Unlike field-based assessment, which is limited by time, labor, and accessibility, remote sensing provides continuous spatial information on vegetation, land cover, moisture conditions, and seasonal agricultural changes. For this reason, both satellite and UAV imagery are widely used in modern agriculture, especially where rapid assessment and site-specific analysis are needed.
One of the main advantages of remote sensing is its ability to capture spatial variability within agricultural land. Tsouros et al. (2019) show that UAV-based applications in precision agriculture can support crop monitoring, weed detection, irrigation assessment, disease observation, and yield estimation. Their review suggests that remotely sensed imagery is valuable not only because it provides visual information, but also because it supports site-specific management. In this way, remote sensing contributes to the shift from uniform farmland treatment to more precise and data-driven agricultural practices.
Satellite imagery adds another important advantage by enabling repeated observation of the same agricultural areas over time. Choudhary et al. (2019) demonstrate that NDVI time-series derived from Sentinel and Landsat imagery can be used to monitor agricultural phenology and identify seasonal crop-development patterns. Moomen et al. (2024) also emphasize that remote sensing has strong potential for sustainable agriculture because it supports large-scale monitoring of land and crop conditions. Together, these studies show that remote sensing is important not only for spatial analysis, but also for temporal monitoring of agricultural change.
The literature further suggests that multi-source imagery can improve environmental interpretation. Wang et al. (2024), for example, combined Sentinel-1 SAR imagery, Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery, and spectral indices in a multi-source remote sensing framework to improve land-cover classification and environmental interpretation. Although their study is not focused directly on crop monitoring, it demonstrates the methodological value of integrating different data sources. Therefore, remote sensing provides a strong foundation for farmland analysis, especially when it is combined with other methods such as vegetation indices, preprocessing techniques, and machine learning models.
2.3 Vegetation Indices and Image Preprocessing
Vegetation indices and preprocessing are essential in agricultural remote sensing because raw imagery usually cannot be used directly for reliable farmland analysis. Instead, spectral bands are corrected, filtered, and transformed into indicators that better represent vegetation condition, crop development, and moisture-related patterns. This is important because farmland image analysis depends not only on access to satellite data, but also on the ability to extract meaningful information from that data consistently.
Among vegetation indices, NDVI is one of the most widely used because it reflects the relationship between red and near-infrared reflectance, which is closely linked to plant activity. Choudhary et al. (2019) showed that NDVI time-series derived from Sentinel and Landsat imagery can be used to monitor agricultural phenology and distinguish farmland according to crop health and seasonal development. At the same time, NDVI is not always sufficient on its own. Wang et al. (2024) demonstrated the value of integrating multiple spectral indices within a multi-source remote sensing framework, which suggests that agricultural and environmental conditions are often too complex to be represented by a single indicator.
The reliability of vegetation indices also depends heavily on preprocessing quality. Clouds, shadows, atmospheric effects, and temporal inconsistencies can all reduce the usefulness of spectral analysis. Magno et al. (2021) addressed this issue directly by developing AgroShadow, a cloud-shadow detection method for Sentinel-2 imagery, showing that shadow correction is important for improving the reliability of later agricultural interpretation. This is especially relevant for farmland monitoring because even strong analytical models may produce unreliable results if the input imagery has not been properly prepared.
Preprocessing is also important from a systems perspective. As satellite-based agricultural monitoring increases in scale, manual image preparation becomes increasingly impractical. Pandit et al. (2022) demonstrated the importance of automated cloud-based pipelines for downloading, compositing, and processing satellite data for near-real-time agricultural applications. Similarly, Tsouros et al. (2019) showed that in UAV-based agricultural workflows, data correction and feature extraction generally take place before classification or prediction. Overall, the literature shows that vegetation indices and preprocessing are fundamental to farmland image analysis and are most effective when integrated into larger analytical systems, including scalable satellite-processing environments such as those used in the present study.
2.4 Machine Learning and Deep Learning for Farmland Image Analysis
Machine learning and deep learning have become important tools in farmland image analysis because they can identify spatial patterns in remotely sensed imagery more effectively than traditional rule-based methods. Agricultural imagery often reflects differences in crop type, growth stage, soil condition, moisture, and surrounding land use, which makes image interpretation a complex task. For this reason, learning-based methods are valuable because they can extract patterns directly from image data and support classification and segmentation in variable agricultural environments.
Previous research shows that both machine learning and deep learning can perform well in remote-sensing classification. Wijesingha et al. (2024) evaluated Random Forest and a two-dimensional convolutional neural network for agricultural land-cover mapping using Landsat archive data and found that both models achieved strong results in the reference setting. However, their study also showed that performance decreased when the models were transferred across different years and regions, which indicates that accuracy alone is not enough for judging practical usefulness. This is especially important for farmland image analysis, since agricultural conditions vary across time and place, and models must remain reliable beyond the original training data.
Deep learning studies further suggest that convolutional neural networks are especially useful for remote-sensing interpretation. Ye et al. (2022) proposed a lightweight VGG-U-Net model for remote sensing image classification and reported about 97% accuracy on EuroSAT, while also reducing memory usage to 3.4 MB. This shows that effective classification does not always require very large models. In a related direction, Kumar et al. (2025) developed a residual CNN for aerial image classification and achieved 96.7% accuracy with an AUC of 0.98, while also reducing training time through cloud-based resources. Although this study focused on aerial cactus identification rather than farmland monitoring directly, it provides useful evidence that scalable deep learning systems can combine strong performance with computational efficiency. Wang et al. (2024) also support this broader methodological direction by showing that machine learning can be strengthened through the integration of SAR imagery, optical imagery, and spectral indices in a multi-source classification framework.
Overall, the literature indicates that machine learning and deep learning are highly effective for image-based agricultural interpretation, particularly in classification tasks. At the same time, these studies show that model performance should be evaluated not only by accuracy, but also by generalization, efficiency, and suitability for real-world deployment. This is highly relevant to the present study, because farmland image analysis requires models that are not only accurate in controlled conditions, but also practical for broader use in remote-sensing-based agricultural monitoring. At the same time, much of the successful deep learning literature in agriculture still relies on close-range plant images rather than satellite-based farmland imagery, which limits its direct relevance to large-scale farmland monitoring.
2.5 Transfer Learning in Agricultural Remote Sensing
Transfer learning has become increasingly important in agricultural remote sensing because labeled geospatial datasets are often limited, expensive to produce, and strongly dependent on local environmental conditions. In such situations, models trained on one dataset or image domain can provide useful prior knowledge for a new target task, reducing the need to train entirely from scratch. This is especially relevant in agriculture, where imagery varies across seasons, landscapes, crop types, and sensor platforms, while high-quality reference data remain difficult to obtain. As a result, transfer learning is not only a technical improvement, but also a practical strategy for making agricultural image analysis more feasible under real data constraints.
A particularly important example is provided by Yu et al. (2022), who proposed a deep transfer learning method for terrace identification in high-resolution remote-sensing imagery. Their study is highly relevant because it addresses a common agricultural remote-sensing problem: the target dataset is small, but accurate pixel-level extraction is still required. Using transfer learning from a larger source dataset to a smaller GF-2 target dataset, the authors reported average results of 93.12% overall accuracy, 91.40% F1 score, and 89.90% MIoU, outperforming direct training. This makes the study a strong methodological example of how transfer learning can improve remote-sensing analysis when labeled samples are limited. However, the application remains focused on terrace detection rather than broader farmland-condition monitoring or crop-health analysis.
A related perspective is offered by Gao et al. (2024), who studied cross-domain transfer learning for weed segmentation and mapping in precision farming using ground and UAV imagery. Their work shows that transfer learning can help bridge differences between image domains and improve agricultural mapping when data come from different sensors, scales, or acquisition settings. This is important because agricultural remote sensing often involves domain shift rather than perfectly uniform data conditions. At the same time, Gao et al. focus on weed mapping rather than satellite-based farmland analysis, and their transfer takes place between ground and UAV images rather than between local imagery and broader satellite-derived farmland imagery. Their study therefore provides strong evidence for cross-domain adaptation, but it also illustrates that transfer learning in agriculture is still often applied to narrower tasks.
The broader problem of transferability is also highlighted by Wijesingha et al. (2024), although their paper is not a transfer-learning study in the strict deep-learning sense. Instead, it examines whether agricultural land-cover models trained on Landsat archive data remain reliable when transferred across different years and regions. The results show a clear reduction in performance under temporal, spatial, and especially spatiotemporal transfer scenarios, which indicates that generalization remains a major challenge in remote-sensing-based agricultural analysis. Supportive evidence from Ye et al. (2022) and Kumar et al. (2025) further suggests that lightweight architectures and scalable cloud-based training can improve the practicality of such systems, but they do not directly solve the transfer-learning problem itself. Overall, the literature shows that transfer learning is highly promising in agricultural remote sensing, particularly under limited-data conditions, but it is still rarely integrated into broader, user-oriented systems for full farmland monitoring and geospatial analysis.
2.6 Web-Based and Cloud-Based Agricultural Monitoring Systems
The growing use of remote sensing and artificial intelligence in agriculture has made system design and delivery increasingly important. Accurate models and high-quality imagery are valuable only when they can be integrated into platforms that are scalable, accessible, and usable for non-specialist users. For this reason, recent research has moved beyond model development alone and has begun to address cloud infrastructure, web delivery, geospatial web processing, and real-time execution as essential components of agricultural monitoring systems.
One of the main advantages of cloud-based systems is scalability. Pandit et al. (2022) developed an automated satellite downloading and processing pipeline on AWS using Sentinel-2 data, Amazon S3 for storing raster outputs, and EC2 for computation. Their system generated cloud-free GeoTIFF outputs, supported GeoServer-based publication, and was designed for cost-effective, efficient, and scalable near-real-time agricultural applications. Kumar et al. (2025) support this broader point from the model-training side by showing that cloud resources can improve the efficiency of deep learning workflows for aerial image classification. Together, these studies suggest that large-scale agricultural image analysis increasingly depends on cloud infrastructure for storage, processing, and operational repeatability.
Web delivery is equally important because it makes agricultural analytics more accessible. Anggraini et al. (2024) show that CNN-based plant disease classification can be deployed through AWS-supported services, while the AgriCure study by Shome et al. presents a cloud-based deep learning platform for automated crop disease detection. Although both studies focus on close-range plant images rather than satellite-based farmland monitoring, they are still useful because they demonstrate that agricultural AI can be translated into user-facing systems rather than remaining only as isolated experimental models. A related but more general contribution comes from Shahid et al. (2023), who show that raster processing and visualization can increasingly be handled in web-based environments. This is relevant to farmland monitoring because georeferenced imagery and raster outputs often need to be delivered through map-based interfaces rather than through desktop-only tools.
System architecture also matters. Marković et al. (2024) explored a cloud-fog image-processing system for smart agriculture and showed that moving part of the computation closer to the data source can improve responsiveness. Antora et al. (2023) similarly proposed an FPGA-based field monitoring prototype for real-time image processing, highlighting the role of hardware efficiency in agricultural systems. These studies are important because they show that agricultural monitoring depends not only on models, but also on how processing is distributed and delivered. At the same time, most existing work still focuses on separate components such as cloud pipelines, web-based deployment, raster visualization, or real-time execution. As a result, the literature supports the importance of web delivery, cloud processing, and geospatial tools, but it also shows the continuing need for an integrated system that combines satellite imagery, transfer learning, and geodata within a single web-based farmland monitoring service.
2.7 Research Gap
The reviewed literature demonstrates that important progress has already been made in several areas related to digital agriculture. Remote sensing studies show that satellite and UAV imagery can support crop monitoring, land-cover classification, phenology analysis, and vegetation assessment. Research on vegetation indices and preprocessing further indicates that spectral indicators such as NDVI are useful for interpreting vegetation dynamics, while image correction procedures such as cloud and shadow removal improve the reliability of agricultural analysis. In addition, studies on machine learning and deep learning confirm that convolutional neural networks and related models can perform effectively in classification and segmentation tasks. Transfer learning has also been shown to be especially valuable when labeled datasets are limited, while cloud-based and web-based systems demonstrate that agricultural analytics can be automated and delivered through digital infrastructures.
However, the literature also reveals several important limitations. Many remote sensing studies focus mainly on analytical methods rather than on practical systems that can be accessed by end users. A large proportion of deep learning research in agriculture relies on close-range images of leaves or plants rather than satellite-based farmland imagery, which reduces its relevance for large-scale land monitoring. Other remote sensing studies tend to concentrate on specific tasks, such as vegetation monitoring, terrace extraction, or weed mapping, rather than broader farmland-condition analysis. Similarly, studies on cloud-based or web-based systems often emphasize infrastructure, automation, or visualization without fully integrating these aspects with advanced remote sensing analysis. As a result, the current body of research remains fragmented, with limited work combining remote sensing, transfer learning, geodata, and deployment technologies into one unified farmland-analysis system.
Therefore, an important gap in the existing literature is the absence of an integrated and user-oriented web service that combines satellite-based farmland imagery, transfer learning, and geodata for practical agricultural analysis. The present study aims to address this gap by developing a web service for analyzing farmland images through transfer learning while incorporating geospatial information into the analytical workflow. In this way, the research is positioned at the intersection of remote sensing, artificial intelligence, and digital agricultural systems, with the aim of moving from separate analytical approaches toward a more accessible and functional tool for farmland monitoring.

Chapter 3. Methodology
3.1. Research Approach and Paradigm
This study adopts an applied, system-oriented research paradigm integrating machine learning, remote sensing data processing, and interactive web-based visualization for the purpose of automated agricultural land monitoring. The primary research objective is the design, implementation, and empirical evaluation of a functional end-to-end analytical platform — rather than the derivation of novel theoretical algorithms — and the study is therefore characterised as applied research in the discipline of geospatial informatics and agricultural data science.
The experimental dimension of the research involves the controlled training and evaluation of a deep learning classification model, the sequential integration of geospatial processing components into a unified backend pipeline, and the systematic assessment of system performance under realistic operational conditions. This experimental orientation is intended to provide an empirical basis for assessing both the technical feasibility and the practical utility of the proposed solution.
The methodological framework is governed by three overarching principles that are maintained consistently throughout all phases of the project:
• Reproducibility. All data sources, model architectures, training procedures, and processing libraries are publicly available, openly licensed, and well-documented. This ensures that the research can be independently verified, replicated, and extended by third parties without reliance on proprietary infrastructure.
• Modularity. The system is architected as a collection of loosely coupled, independently deployable components — a machine learning classification module, a geospatial processing backend, and a frontend user interface — each of which exposes well-defined interfaces and can be developed, tested, and improved in isolation.
• Scalability. All architectural decisions are made with future extensibility in mind, supporting the prospective integration of additional satellite data sources, expanded land-cover classification taxonomies, and broader geographic coverage beyond the prototype's current scope.
It is explicitly noted that this study does not seek to surpass state-of-the-art benchmark accuracy on remote sensing classification tasks. Its scientific contribution lies in demonstrating the feasibility and practical value of integrating transfer learning, cloud-based satellite data retrieval, geospatial raster processing, and interactive web technologies into a unified, accessible analytical platform for agricultural monitoring — a combination that the existing literature addresses only partially in end-to-end form.
3.2. System Architecture
The developed system follows a three-tier modular architecture consisting of a frontend interface layer, a backend processing layer, and an embedded machine learning module. This separation of concerns is intended to allow each tier to be maintained, tested, and scaled independently, while a well-defined RESTful API contract governs all inter-layer communication.
3.2.1. Frontend Layer
The frontend is implemented using React (v18), a component-based JavaScript library that enables the construction of dynamic, responsive user interfaces through a virtual DOM rendering mechanism. React was selected over alternative frameworks such as Vue.js and Angular on the basis of its widespread adoption in production-grade web applications, its mature ecosystem of complementary libraries, and its efficient reconciliation algorithm, which is particularly advantageous for applications that require frequent map-based state updates in response to user interactions.
Geospatial map rendering is handled by Leaflet.js, an open-source JavaScript library for interactive web cartography. Leaflet enables efficient rendering of tile-based satellite basemap layers sourced from Sentinel Hub endpoints, vector overlay layers derived from user-uploaded GeoJSON field boundary files, and colour-coded analytical result layers produced by the backend. Its lightweight runtime footprint and compatibility with a broad range of tile providers make it well-suited to the requirements of this web service.
Users interact with the interface to define geographic areas of interest, upload field boundary files in GeoJSON format, initiate analysis requests, and inspect classification and vegetation index outputs returned by the backend as structured result overlays.
3.2.2. Backend Layer
The backend is developed in Python using the FastAPI framework, which serves as the central orchestration layer responsible for routing all incoming HTTP requests, coordinating data flow between system components, and managing communication with external satellite data platforms. FastAPI was selected over alternative Python web frameworks — notably Flask and Django REST Framework — due to its superior request throughput for I/O-bound operations, its native support for asynchronous coroutine-based request handling via Python's asyncio library, and its automatic generation of interactive, standards-compliant API documentation through the OpenAPI specification.
Geospatial vector data operations — including the validation, area computation, and centroid extraction of user-uploaded field boundary polygons — are performed using GeoPandas. Raster data manipulation — including spatial clipping of satellite imagery to parcel extents and pixel-wise spectral band arithmetic for vegetation index calculation — is performed using Rasterio, a Python library built on the GDAL abstraction layer.
3.2.3. Machine Learning Module
The machine learning module is responsible for the land-use and land-cover (LULC) classification of satellite image patches. It encapsulates the pretrained and fine-tuned ResNet-50 model, exposes a prediction interface callable by the backend processing pipeline, and manages the sequence of preprocessing transformations required to convert raw raster data into model-compatible input tensors. The module is implemented using the PyTorch deep learning framework and operates as an integrated component of the backend rather than as a separately deployed microservice, simplifying deployment and reducing inter-component communication overhead in the current prototype implementation.
3.2.4. System Interaction Pattern
The interaction between these three layers follows a request-driven pattern. A user action on the frontend — such as uploading a GeoJSON file and submitting an analysis request — triggers an HTTP POST request to the backend API. The backend retrieves the corresponding satellite imagery from Google Earth Engine, performs geospatial processing and vegetation index computation, invokes the machine learning module for land-cover classification inference, and returns a structured JSON response. The frontend receives the response and updates the map visualization layer and the statistics panel accordingly without requiring a full page reload.
3.3. Data Processing Workflow
The data processing workflow is designed as a sequential, five-stage pipeline intended to transform raw satellite imagery and user-supplied geospatial field boundaries into agricultural analysis outputs. Each stage is defined with specified inputs, outputs, and processing responsibilities. In the current prototype implementation, stages are partially realised using mock responses, providing a structural scaffold for the intended full pipeline. The following description documents both the architectural design of the intended system and the current prototype status of each stage.
Stage 1 — Satellite Imagery Retrieval
Intended design: Multispectral satellite imagery is to be retrieved from the Google Earth Engine (GEE) cloud platform via the earthengine-api Python client. Image collection queries will be parameterised by the geographic bounding box of the user-uploaded field boundaries, a user-specified temporal range, and a maximum cloud coverage threshold. A median composite will be generated over the specified time window to produce a spatially complete, cloud-minimised raster for the area of interest.
Current prototype status: The SatelliteService class returns simulated satellite metadata — including dataset name, acquisition date, and spatial resolution — without performing any real API call to GEE or Sentinel Hub. Integration of actual imagery retrieval is planned as the primary extension in subsequent development iterations.
Stage 2 — Spatial Clipping to Field Boundaries
Intended design: The retrieved raster image is to be spatially clipped to the boundaries of each individual agricultural parcel using the Rasterio library's mask function, retaining only pixel values falling within the uploaded parcel polygon and assigning no-data values to all external pixels.
Current prototype status: The geoprocess_raster_input function contains a commented placeholder for real Rasterio-based clipping logic. In the absence of a real raster path, the function returns a mock status object, allowing the pipeline to proceed without raising errors.
Stage 3 — Vegetation Index Computation
Intended design: NDVI and EVI are to be computed from the clipped multispectral raster data using pixel-wise band arithmetic implemented via NumPy, using the formulas:
NDVI = (NIR − RED) / (NIR + RED)
EVI = 2.5 × (NIR − RED) / (NIR + 6·RED − 7.5·BLUE + 1)
Current prototype status: The calculate_advanced_indices function currently returns a hardcoded mock value of 0.72 for NDVI. The mathematical formulas and their spectral band dependencies are fully specified in the code annotations, pending the availability of real clipped raster data from Stage 2.
Stage 4 — Image Preprocessing for Model Inference
Intended design: Each clipped image patch is to be resized to 224×224 pixels, normalised using ImageNet statistics (mean: [0.485, 0.456, 0.406]; std: [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]), and converted to a PyTorch tensor of shape (1, C, H, W) for single-sample inference. This preprocessing pipeline mirrors the val_test_transform used during model training.
Current prototype status: This preprocessing pipeline is fully defined and validated in the training notebook. In the backend prototype, it is not yet invoked during inference because real raster data does not reach this stage; integration is anticipated alongside Stage 2 development.
Stage 5 — Model Inference and Result Delivery
Intended design: The preprocessed tensor is passed through the fine-tuned ResNet-50 model, which produces raw output logits over the 10 EuroSAT land-cover classes. The predicted class is determined by applying argmax on the raw logits. The predicted class label is returned together with computed NDVI and EVI values as a structured JSON response.
Current prototype status: The run_ml_analysis_pipeline function returns a hardcoded mock result — crop_type: 'Winter Wheat', confidence: 0.84, ndvi_value: 0.72 — enabling end-to-end demonstration of the API response schema and frontend visualization. The trained ResNet-50 weights (best_resnet_satellite_model.pth) are available and will be integrated into the backend inference pipeline in the subsequent development phase.
3.4. Machine Learning Model
3.4.1. Architecture Selection: ResNet-50
The machine learning component of the system employs a transfer learning strategy based on the ResNet-50 convolutional neural network (He et al., 2016), which was pretrained on the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge (ILSVRC) dataset comprising approximately 1.28 million labelled images across 1,000 object classes.
ResNet-50 is a 50-layer deep convolutional neural network organised into four residual stages, each composed of a sequence of Bottleneck residual blocks. Each bottleneck block applies three successive convolutions — a 1×1 dimensionality reduction convolution, a 3×3 spatial convolution, and a 1×1 dimensionality expansion convolution — and combines the result with a residual shortcut connection that adds the block's input directly to its output. These shortcut connections mitigate the vanishing gradient problem and allow deeper layers to refine existing representations incrementally rather than learning them from scratch.
The hierarchical depth of ResNet-50 allows the network to learn a nested hierarchy of feature representations of increasing abstraction: early layers encode low-level spectral responses and textural gradients, intermediate layers capture structural patterns, and deep layers encode high-level semantic representations. These characteristics make ResNet-50 particularly well-suited for satellite image classification tasks, where accurate discrimination between spectrally similar land-cover classes requires the integration of nuanced, multi-scale visual information.
3.4.2. Transfer Learning Strategy and Fine-Tuning
The transfer learning procedure follows a full-network fine-tuning strategy in which the entire set of pretrained convolutional weights is updated during training. The final fully connected classification layer of the original model — a linear layer mapping from 2048 input features to 1000 ImageNet classes — is replaced with a new linear layer producing a 10-dimensional output corresponding to the 10 land-cover classes of the EuroSAT dataset. The implementation detail is provided in Appendix A.
This head replacement is necessary because the target classification taxonomy (EuroSAT, 10 classes) is fundamentally different from the source taxonomy (ImageNet, 1000 classes). Training then proceeds over the full network, allowing the pretrained convolutional representations to be adapted to the spectral and textural characteristics of Sentinel-2 multispectral satellite imagery while retaining the generalizable low-level feature detectors acquired during ImageNet pretraining.
3.4.3. Training Configuration
Training is performed over 5 epochs on the EuroSAT training subset. The number of training epochs was set to 5, as validation accuracy plateaued after Epoch 3, indicating convergence of the fine-tuning process within this compact training schedule. The Adam optimizer (Kingma & Ba, 2015) is employed for parameter updates. Adam maintains exponentially weighted moving averages of both the first moment (mean) and the second moment (uncentred variance) of per-parameter gradient histories, using these estimates to compute an adaptive effective learning rate for each parameter independently. The cross-entropy loss function measures the divergence between the predicted class probability distribution and the true one-hot class label vector, providing a theoretically well-motivated objective for multi-class classification problems.
3.4.4. Data Augmentation and Normalisation
The training dataset is subject to a stochastic data augmentation pipeline designed to improve the model's ability to generalise to the photometric and geometric variability present in real-world satellite imagery. Augmentations applied to the training split include:
• Random horizontal flipping (applied with probability 0.5)
• Random rotation within a ±10° angular range
Augmentation is applied exclusively to the training subset; the validation and test subsets are processed using a deterministic transformation pipeline consisting only of spatial resizing and normalisation, ensuring unbiased evaluation metrics.
3.4.5. Dataset Partitioning
The EuroSAT dataset (27,000 labelled samples across 10 classes) is partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets using stratified random sampling with an 80:10:10 ratio, following the approach described in Cochran (1977) for proportional allocation in stratified designs. Stratified sampling ensures that the relative class proportions are preserved across all three subsets, reducing the risk of partition-induced class imbalance. The partitioning is implemented using sklearn.model_selection.train_test_split with stratify=labels and a fixed random_state=42 for reproducibility.

3.6. Evaluation Approach
The evaluation of the system is conducted at two complementary levels: the quantitative assessment of machine learning model performance, and the functional correctness validation of the integrated end-to-end web service.
3.6.1. Model Evaluation
Classification performance is evaluated on the held-out test subset of the EuroSAT dataset (2,700 samples, 10% of the total, selected via stratified sampling), which was withheld from both training and validation and is therefore unseen by the model during any phase of the training procedure. The following quantitative evaluation metrics are computed:
• Overall Accuracy (OA): the proportion of correctly classified samples across all classes. While OA provides a general aggregate indicator of model performance, it may be misleading in the presence of class imbalance and is therefore reported together with per-class metrics.
• Per-class Precision: the proportion of true positive predictions among all samples predicted as belonging to a given class, measuring the model's propensity for false positive errors.
• Per-class Recall: the proportion of true positive predictions among all ground-truth samples of a given class, measuring the model's propensity for false negative errors.
• F1-Score: the harmonic mean of precision and recall for each class, providing a balanced scalar summary of per-class classification quality. The macro-averaged and weighted-averaged F1-scores are reported to account for class size imbalances.
• Confusion Matrix: a C×C matrix (where C = 10) reporting the number of samples from each true class predicted to belong to each predicted class. Confusion matrix analysis identifies the class pairs most susceptible to misclassification — for example, Annual Crop versus Permanent Crop — and provides diagnostic information for guiding potential improvements to the training data or model architecture.
3.6.2. System-Level Evaluation
The functional evaluation of the integrated web service assesses the correctness and consistency of the API pipeline and frontend integration. In the current prototype, system-level evaluation focuses on the following criteria:
• API schema correctness: All endpoints return JSON responses conforming to the defined schema with correct HTTP status codes, verified using both automated HTTP client tests and the auto-generated FastAPI interactive documentation (/docs).
• Frontend integration: The React frontend correctly consumes the JSON response and renders classification results and vegetation index values as map overlay annotations and statistics panel updates.
• Pipeline structural completeness: Each of the five pipeline stages is represented by an independently callable function with a defined interface, such that mock implementations can be replaced by real implementations without altering the surrounding pipeline logic or API contract.
Full end-to-end validation with live satellite imagery retrieval, Rasterio-based spatial clipping, computed NDVI/EVI values, and live ResNet-50 inference is planned as the principal functional evaluation task in the subsequent development phase, once the GEE integration and model deployment are completed.
3.7. Summary
The methodology presented in this chapter defines a comprehensive and reproducible framework for integrating transfer learning-based satellite image classification, cloud-based geospatial data processing, and interactive web-based visualization into a unified analytical platform for automated agricultural land monitoring. The three-tier system architecture enforces a clear separation of concerns between the user interface layer, the backend processing layer, and the machine learning module, supporting independent development and testing of each component.
The five-stage data processing pipeline — satellite imagery retrieval via GEE, spatial clipping via Rasterio, vegetation index computation, image preprocessing, and ResNet-50 inference — is specified at the architectural level and documented per stage with its intended design and current implementation status. The trained ResNet-50 model, which achieved a peak validation accuracy of 98.56% on the EuroSAT benchmark, is available for backend integration and represents the primary machine learning contribution of this research. Full integration of real satellite data retrieval and model inference is planned as the main objective of the subsequent development phase.
This approach — treating the validated machine learning model and the web platform architecture as distinct but related contributions — is broadly consistent with practice in applied systems research, where individual components are often evaluated independently before full end-to-end integration.

7 Technology Comparison and Selection Rationale
7.1 Introduction
The selection of appropriate technologies represents a crucial stage in the design of modern data-driven systems, as it directly influences system performance, scalability, maintainability, and long-term adaptability. In the case of the developed farmland analysis platform, multiple technological alternatives were considered across several system layers, including backend development, machine learning, geospatial data processing, satellite data access, and frontend visualization.
Rather than relying on a single criterion, the selection process was guided by a combination of factors, including computational efficiency, support for asynchronous execution, compatibility with the Python-based ecosystem, availability of pretrained models and geospatial datasets, as well as the maturity of documentation and community support. This multi-criteria evaluation approach allows for a more balanced and practically justified selection of technologies.
The purpose of this section is to provide a comparative analysis of the considered technologies and to justify the final design decisions adopted in the system.
7.2 Backend Framework
The backend layer functions as the central coordination component of the system, handling API requests, orchestrating geospatial data processing, and managing communication with external services. Among the evaluated options, FastAPI, Flask, and Django REST Framework represent commonly used Python-based frameworks.
Flask is often considered a flexible and lightweight solution, particularly suitable for simple applications. However, its default synchronous execution model may introduce limitations in scenarios involving multiple concurrent I/O operations, such as satellite data retrieval and file processing. While asynchronous extensions are available, their integration may increase system complexity.
Django REST Framework provides a more comprehensive environment with built-in features such as authentication and ORM integration. Nevertheless, for a relatively lightweight analytical service, its monolithic structure may introduce unnecessary overhead and reduce overall system efficiency.
In contrast, FastAPI offers native asynchronous request handling based on Python’s asyncio, which can be particularly advantageous for I/O-bound workloads. In addition, its automatic generation of OpenAPI-compliant documentation and strict request validation using Pydantic models contribute to improved development efficiency and reliability. For these reasons, FastAPI appears to provide a suitable balance between performance and development simplicity for the given system.

7.3 Machine Learning Framework
The machine learning component requires a framework that supports transfer learning workflows, efficient model experimentation, and integration with pretrained architectures. Among the considered frameworks, PyTorch, TensorFlow, and Keras are among the most widely adopted.
TensorFlow is commonly used in production environments due to its scalability and deployment capabilities. Keras, as a high-level interface, simplifies model development but may limit flexibility when more detailed control over training procedures is required.
PyTorch, by contrast, is widely recognised for its dynamic computation graph, which enables more intuitive debugging and flexible model design. This characteristic is particularly beneficial in research-oriented tasks such as transfer learning and fine-tuning of pretrained models. Furthermore, the availability of pretrained architectures through the torchvision library facilitates rapid experimentation and reduces implementation complexity.
Based on these considerations, PyTorch was selected as it provides a favourable balance between flexibility, usability, and compatibility with the research-oriented nature of the project.

7.4 Geospatial Processing Tools
The system requires handling both vector and raster geospatial data. For this purpose, GeoPandas, Rasterio, and GDAL were evaluated.
GDAL provides a comprehensive and powerful low-level interface for geospatial data processing. However, its complexity and relatively steep learning curve may pose challenges in rapid prototyping contexts.
GeoPandas offers a high-level, user-friendly interface for vector data manipulation, building upon familiar Pandas data structures. It supports essential operations such as geometry validation, coordinate transformations, and spatial analysis, which are required in the system.
Rasterio, which is built on top of GDAL, provides a more accessible interface for raster data operations, including spatial clipping, masking, and band extraction. Its integration with NumPy enables efficient numerical processing of raster data.
Thus, the combined use of GeoPandas and Rasterio allows leveraging the functionality of GDAL indirectly while maintaining a more intuitive and development-friendly interface.



7.5 Satellite Data Platforms
Access to satellite imagery is a fundamental requirement for the system. Two platforms were considered: Google Earth Engine and Sentinel Hub.
Google Earth Engine provides access to extensive satellite datasets and supports large-scale geospatial analysis directly in the cloud. Its ability to perform operations such as filtering, compositing, and cloud masking makes it particularly suitable for backend data processing tasks.
Sentinel Hub, on the other hand, is optimised for efficient delivery of satellite imagery through web APIs, making it well-suited for frontend visualization. While its analytical capabilities are more limited compared to GEE, its performance in real-time rendering is advantageous for interactive applications.
In this context, the combined use of both platforms can be considered a complementary approach, where Google Earth Engine supports analytical processing and Sentinel Hub enables efficient visualization. This division of responsibilities enhances both system performance and usability.

7.6 Frontend Technologies
The frontend component is responsible for user interaction and geospatial data visualization. Among the evaluated frameworks, React, Vue.js, and Angular were considered.
React is widely used due to its component-based architecture and efficient rendering through the virtual DOM. These characteristics support modular development and allow efficient updates of dynamic UI elements, which is particularly relevant for map-based applications.
For geospatial visualization, Leaflet was selected due to its lightweight design, ease of integration, and sufficient functionality for rendering GeoJSON data and satellite imagery layers. Although alternatives such as Mapbox GL JS offer advanced visual capabilities, they may introduce additional complexity or licensing constraints.

7.7 Conclusion
Overall, the comparative analysis suggests that the selected technology stack provides a balanced combination of performance, flexibility, and ease of development. While alternative solutions may offer advantages in specific aspects, the chosen technologies appear to align more closely with the requirements of the proposed system.
Furthermore, the modular nature of the selected tools allows for future extensions and potential replacement of individual components without significant changes to the overall architecture. This indicates that the system design is not only suitable for the current prototype implementation but may also support further development towards a more production-ready solution.
